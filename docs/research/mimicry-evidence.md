**Grading scale used.**

- **A** = strongly documented: directly observed and described in peer-reviewed literature.
- **B** = reported in scientific literature but needs qualification (authors hedge, single observation, later authors dispute or reinterpret).
- **C** = tentative, anecdotal, popular-media only, or not established.

A grade describes how well the *behaviour and its resemblance* are documented. No target has experimental evidence that predators are actually deceived (see section 3). So even grade A means "well-documented resemblance", not "proven function".

## 2. Target-by-target evidence

### 01 — Flatfish / banded sole — Grade A

**Sources and wording**

- [Norman2001], Results (verbatim): "When moving faster (using jet propulsion), the octopus drew all its arms into a leaf-shaped wedge with a central mantle trailing the head (figure 1c). In this form it swam between worm mounds, undulating the body in the fashion of a swimming flatfish. We believe that this unique posture is mimicry of an abundant sole found in this habitat (Zebrias spp., figure 1d)."
- [Norman2001] lists soles among the three impersonations the authors are "confident" about.
- [Hanlon2008] (abstract only; close paraphrase): nearly 500 episodes of mimicry of "a local, abundant flounder" were analysed over five days of natural foraging; both octopus species studied mimicked the shape, swimming actions, speed, duration, and *sometimes* the coloration of swimming flounders. The octopuses were actively moving and conspicuous during flounder mimicry, and camouflaged and motionless or slowly crawling immediately before and after. Flounder mimicry was used only when movement would give away camouflage.
- [Huffard2010] (verbatim), defining the behaviour from Hanlon et al.: "'Flatfish swimming' comprises the following behavioural elements: similar swimming durations and speeds to that of flatfish, arms positioned to attain flatfish shape, both eyes positioned prominently as flatfish eyes, and undulating movements of arms during swimming that resemble fin movements of flatfish (Hanlon et al., 2008)."

**What is documented (posture, arms, colour, movement)**

- Arms: all eight drawn together into a flat, leaf-shaped wedge [Norman2001]; "arms positioned to attain flatfish shape" [Huffard2010].
- Mantle and head: mantle central, trailing the head [Norman2001]; head and mantle lowered, body dorsoventrally compressed; only the eyes raised and prominent, like flatfish eyes [Huffard2010].
- Propulsion: jet propulsion [Norman2001].
- Movement: undulation of the body [Norman2001] / of the arms, resembling flatfish fin movement [Huffard2010]; close to the bottom, between mounds [Norman2001].
- Timing: bursts matching flounder swimming speed and duration [Hanlon2008] (abstract). A secondary summary gives an average burst of about 6 seconds, the same as the flounders [Faulkes2009] (blog summary of Hanlon2008; not verified against the paper).
- Colour: in *T. mimicus*, a high-contrast dark brown and light cream pattern during flatfish swimming — unlike the other flatfish-swimming octopuses, which stay camouflaged [Huffard2010]. Coloration matched flounders only "sometimes" [Hanlon2008] (abstract).

**What is NOT documented (as far as I could read)**

- Exact arm-by-arm arrangement inside the wedge, which arms lead or trail, undulation frequency or amplitude, swimming height above the bottom in cm, and absolute speed. Some of this may be in the body of [Hanlon2008], which I could not open.
- Any statistical comparison of octopus and flatfish shape or colour: [Huffard2010] state that "a lack of standardized photographs precluded our ability to examine the degree of possible mimicry with statistical analysis".

**Proposed model species — this is where the qualification lies**

- [Norman2001]: banded sole, *Zebrias* spp.
- [Hanlon2008] (abstract): "a local, abundant flounder" (species not named in the abstract).
- [Huffard2010]: a *suite* of flatfish rather than one species — the conspicuous toxic peacock sole *Pardachirus pavoninus*, *Zebrias* spp., and "a suite of drably coloured non-toxic flounders and soles", with the common flounders *Bothus mancus* and *B. pantherinus* overlapping in range. They describe it as "facultative imperfect mimicry of conspicuous and/or inconspicuous models, both toxic and non-toxic (Soleidae and Bothidae)".
- So "banded sole (*Zebrias*)" is one research group's proposed model. A site label of "flatfish" is safer than "zebra sole".

**Context**: used when crossing open sand, i.e. when the animal must move and cannot stay camouflaged [Hanlon2008] (abstract). Not reported as a response to a specific attacker.

**Criticism / alternatives**

- [Huffard2010]: the lack of a conclusive flatfish model "has generally been identified as a weakness in the cephalopod mimicry literature (Hanlon et al., 2008)"; they call flatfish swimming "the most widely publicized though insufficiently analysed example of conspicuous defence in octopuses".
- Dorsoventrally compressed swimming may simply be an efficient way for a long-armed octopus to swim (lift generation), independent of mimicry [Huffard2010]. *Wunderpus* and *Abdopus aculeatus* swim flattened without the flatfish elements.
- Flatfish swimming is not unique to this species: also in *Macrotritopus defilippi* (Atlantic), the "White V" octopus and the Hawaiian long-armed sand octopus [Huffard2010]; [Hanlon2010] (abstract only).
- A blog summary reports that in the 2008 study *T. mimicus* often did not show a flounder-like colour pattern while swimming this way, and that the second species at the site resembled the local flounders more closely [Faulkes2009] (secondary; not verified).
- Whether the model is toxic matters for the "Batesian" label; see section 3.

**Animation note**: body shape, eye position, arm undulation and banded colour are documented. Fine timing and exact arm layout would be reconstruction unless the body of [Hanlon2008] or the published video is consulted.

### 02 — Lionfish (*Pterois*) — Grade B

**Sources and wording**

- [Norman2001], Results (verbatim): "Four individuals were also observed swimming just above the sea-floor with arms trailing from the body, taking on the appearance of a lion-fish (Pterois spp., figure 1f) swimming with its banded poisonous spines fully flared. Figure 1e is the closest image we have to this behaviour."
- [Norman2001] include lionfish in the three impersonations they are "confident" about; two individuals showed all three.
- [Huffard2010] (verbatim): "Conspicuously bold arm bands may enable the mimicry of banded sea kraits and lionfish by these two octopuses, and toxic soles by T. mimicus (Norman et al., 2001)." Note "may", and that the sentence refers to both *T. mimicus* and *Wunderpus*. Lionfish is absent from the list of models in their Introduction.
- [AMNH2015] (not peer reviewed): "To imitate this fish, the octopus splays its arms."

**What is documented**

- Position: swimming just above the sea floor.
- Arms: "trailing from the body"; banded.
- Number of animals: four individuals.

**What is NOT documented**

- No clear photograph: the authors themselves say Fig. 1e is only "the closest image we have".
- Arm arrangement in any detail (how many arms out, radial or swept back, how far spread), mantle shape, swimming speed, duration, direction of travel, and what triggered it. The popular description of arms held out radially like a fan of spines comes from outreach material and Wikipedia (uncited there), not from the 2001 text, which says "trailing".
- No context: no predator or stimulus is named.
- No independent peer-reviewed confirmation found. [Hanlon2008]'s abstract does not mention lionfish.

**Model**: *Pterois* spp. (genus level only).

**Criticism / alternatives**

- Roger Hanlon was quoted in 2002 as saying that only flatfish mimicry had been proved conclusively [CSM2002] (newspaper; secondary).
- A 2011 Scientific American feature describes most of the octopus's impressions other than the flounder as remaining speculative [Forbes2011] (magazine).
- General caveat from the original authors: "Human interpretations of potential mimicries are subjective" [Norman2001].

**Animation note**: largely a conceptual reconstruction. The only documented elements are "swimming just above the bottom, banded arms trailing".

### 03 — Banded sea snake / sea krait (*Laticauda*) — Grade A for the display, B for the interpretation

**Sources and wording**

- [Norman2001], Results (verbatim): "On four occasions, attacks by small territorial damselfishes (Amphiprion spp.) elicited a posture where six arms were threaded down a hole and two were raised in opposite directions, banded, curled and undulated (figure 1g), to produce the appearance of a banded sea-snake (Laticauda sp.) (figure 1h and Quicktime clip in electronic Appendix A)."
- [Norman2001], Discussion (verbatim): "on all occasions when sea-snake mimicry was observed it was exclusively a reaction to an attack by territorial damselfishes. Sea-snakes forage by entering burrows, and are predators of damselfishes (M. Norman, personal observation)."
- [Huffard2010] (verbatim, Introduction): the high-contrast pattern is "a behaviour believed to represent facultative imperfect (low-fidelity) mimicry of a visually conspicuous venomous sea snake [Laticauda spp.]". Note "believed" and "imperfect (low-fidelity)".

**What is documented**

- Arms: six threaded down a hole/burrow; two raised/extended in opposite directions.
- Colour: the two exposed arms banded (black-and-white bands are discussed in the paper).
- Movement: the two arms curled and undulated.
- Context: four occasions; each time in response to attack by small territorial pomacentrids (anemonefish, *Amphiprion* spp.), and never in another context.
- Media: photograph (Fig. 1g) and a video clip in the electronic appendix.

**What is NOT documented**

- What the mantle and head do (hidden in the burrow, or exposed and pale?). The 2001 text does not say.
- Duration, undulation rate, and whether the arms are held on the sand or lifted into the water ("raised" is the only word used).
- Whether the damselfish actually retreated. The paper reports what elicited the display, not its outcome.
- The species of *Laticauda*.

**Model**: *Laticauda* sp., banded sea krait (venomous).

**Criticism / alternatives**

- The authors themselves raise convergence: the octopus "may take on the appearance of a sea-snake simply because the same selective forces that make black-and-white bands a useful signal for the sea-snake also apply to the octopus", then judge this "very unlikely" [Norman2001].
- The claim that sea snakes prey on damselfish, which underpins the "chooses the right mimic for the threat" argument, is cited only as a personal observation of the first author [Norman2001].
- [Hanlon2008] (abstract; close paraphrase): in filmed interactions with fishes and stomatopods, typical secondary defence behaviours, not mimicry, were used. This is in tension with the 2001 interpretation of a targeted mimetic response to attacking fish. I could not read whether the 2008 paper discusses the sea-snake display directly.
- All observations come from one research group; n = 4 events; no experiment.
- Banded arms are also the species' resting pattern and part of a general high-contrast display shared with *Wunderpus* [Huffard2010], so banding alone is not specific to snake imitation.

**Animation note**: the core configuration (six arms down a burrow, two banded arms out in opposite directions, undulating; triggered by a damselfish attack) is documented. Mantle position and timing are reconstruction.

### 04 — Stingray — Grade C

- **I could find no peer-reviewed source for this.** It is absent from [Norman2001], from the abstracts of [NormanHochberg2005] and [Hanlon2008], and from [Huffard2010].
- Where it appears: popular lists (for example a 2025 Guinness World Records article naming stingray among "at least 16" animals) and diver/hobbyist pages, without primary citations.
- A hobbyist commentary found in search results argues that the "stingray" form is essentially the flatfish posture with one arm trailing like a tail. That is an informal opinion, but it matches the most economical reading: this is the flatfish-swimming behaviour seen from another angle or with a trailing arm.
- No posture, colour or movement description exists in the scientific literature I could access. No model species is proposed anywhere.
- **Animation note**: entirely a conceptual reconstruction. If kept, it should be labelled as an unverified diver report and visually linked to the flatfish form.

### 05 — Snake eel — Grade C

- **I could find no peer-reviewed source for this.** Absent from every scientific source consulted.
- Appears only in popular lists (same type of source as stingray).
- No description of posture, colour or movement; no model species. One could guess it derives from the banded-arm (sea-snake) display or from a single banded arm emerging from a burrow, but that is my speculation, not a published statement.
- **Animation note**: entirely a conceptual reconstruction; candidate for removal or for merging into the sea-snake entry with an explicit "popularly also described as…" label.

### 06 — Jellyfish — Grade B (weak; borderline C)

**Source and wording**

- [Norman2001], Results (verbatim): "In another incident, a large female (arm span, 60 cm) swam to the sea surface from 4 m deep, then slowly sank with undulating arms spread evenly around the animal. This behaviour may impersonate large jellyfishes found in the region."
- The authors then exclude it from what they are sure of: "at this stage we are only confident in our interpretation of the mimicry of soles, lion-fishes and banded sea-snakes."
- [AMNH2015]: scientists "have also observed behaviors suggesting" a bigger repertoire, and the octopus "may be able to mimic anemones and jellyfish as well".

**What is documented**: one incident, one animal; swam up from 4 m to the surface; then sank slowly; arms spread evenly around the body; arms undulating.

**What is NOT documented**: colour pattern; mantle shape (the "inflated mantle" in popular accounts is not in the paper); pulsing; what triggered the behaviour; any repeat observation; any model species ("large jellyfishes found in the region" is the only wording). No figure.

**Criticism / alternatives**: single observation, hedged by the authors ("may"), no named model, no later peer-reviewed confirmation found. Not mentioned in [Huffard2010]. An octopus swimming to the surface and parachuting down with spread arms could be ordinary locomotion.

**Animation note**: the slow sinking with evenly spread, undulating arms is documented once. Bell-like pulsing, mantle inflation and colour are reconstruction.

### 07 — Sea sponge — Grade B

**Source and wording**

- [Hanlon2008] (abstract only; close paraphrase): when motionless, the octopuses assumed body patterns and postures resembling small sponges, tube-worm tubes, or colonial tunicates, which were among the few objects in the open sand habitat.
- [CSM2002] (newspaper quoting Hanlon): when the octopus stops, "it often gets into the exact shape and color of a sponge, a feather-duster worm, or a sea squirt".
- [Huffard2010] (verbatim, general statement): *T. mimicus* "can regulate conspicuousness while imitating animals (mobile and sessile) or inanimate objects, and frequently challenges the distinction between mimicry and crypsis".

**What is documented**: a stationary resemblance — the animal is motionless; body pattern and posture resemble a small sponge.

**What is NOT documented (to me)**: which posture, arm placement, skin texture or colour produces the sponge resemblance; how often; which sponge. The abstract gives none of this, and I could not read the body of the paper. There is no named model species.

**Criticism / alternatives**

- This is better described as camouflage by resemblance to a background object (masquerade) than as mimicry of a dangerous model. The 2008 abstract's own framing is that the animals were "camouflaged" when motionless; [Huffard2010] note the species blurs mimicry and crypsis.
- The abstract covers two octopus species jointly; it does not say which resemblance was shown by which species.
- No claim of toxicity or deterrence is attached to sponges in this context.

**Animation note**: only "motionless, sponge-like pattern and posture" is supported. Any specific pose is reconstruction. A sponge is not a "dangerous animal" and should not be presented alongside the lionfish and sea snake as predator deterrence.

### 08 — Colonial tunicate — Grade B

- Same single source and wording as 07: [Hanlon2008] (abstract only) — motionless animals resembling "colonial tunicates". [CSM2002] quotes Hanlon saying "sea squirt".
- Documented: stationary resemblance in pattern and posture. Not documented (to me): posture, colour, texture, model species.
- Same alternative reading: camouflage/masquerade against one of the few objects on the sand.
- **Animation note**: conceptual reconstruction apart from "motionless".

### 09 — Tube-dwelling polychaete worm — Grade B

- Same source: [Hanlon2008] (abstract only). **Wording matters**: the abstract says "tube-worm tubes" — the resemblance is to the *tube* standing on the sand, a stationary object, not to a worm with an extended feeding crown. [CSM2002] has Hanlon saying "a feather-duster worm", which is a looser newspaper paraphrase.
- Documented: stationary resemblance. Not documented (to me): how the octopus forms it (for example whether a single arm or the whole body is raised), colour, model species.
- [Norman2001] mentions "worm mounds" only as landscape the octopus swims between; they do not claim worm mimicry.
- Same alternative reading as 07 and 08.
- **Animation note**: an animation showing feathery radioles would go beyond the source. A plain upright tube shape is the most that the abstract supports, and even that pose is inferred.

### 10 — Sea anemone — Grade B (weak)

**Source and wording**

- [Norman2001], Results (verbatim): "Other distinctive behaviours were observed, including sitting on top of sand mounds and raising all the arms above the body, each arm being held in a zigzag form. It is possible that this posture impersonates large solitary sand anemones (such as Megalactis spp.) that are armed with powerful stinging cells (nematocysts)."
- Excluded from the confident list by the authors (same sentence as quoted under 06).
- [AMNH2015]: "may be able to mimic anemones".

**What is documented**: location (on top of a sand mound); all arms raised above the body; each arm held in a zigzag.

**What is NOT documented**: number of observations or individuals; colour pattern; whether the arms move or sway; duration; trigger or context; no figure. The model is offered only as an example ("such as *Megalactis* spp.").

**Criticism / alternatives**: authors' own hedge ("It is possible"); no later peer-reviewed confirmation found; not mentioned in [Huffard2010]. Could be a general alarm/display posture.

**Animation note**: the pose (arms up, zigzag, on a mound) is documented. Tentacle-like swaying, colour and context are reconstruction.

### Other claimed targets not in the list of ten (for completeness)

- **Mantis shrimp**: reported by the divers who first found the animal, per a newspaper [CSM2002]. No peer-reviewed source found. [Hanlon2008]'s abstract mentions stomatopods only as animals the octopus *interacted with* (eliciting secondary defences), not as models.
- **Crab**: Wikipedia carries a claim of crab mimicry to approach prey, tagged "citation needed" [WikipediaMimic]. No source found.
- **Brittle star and others**: popular lists only.
- **Counts of "13", "15", "16" or "18" species**: [Hanlon2008]'s abstract says the octopuses "are reputed to mimic up to 13 species" — reputed, not confirmed. - **Jawfish**: the reverse case — a jawfish (*Stalix* cf. *histrio*) apparently using the octopus's banded arms as cover [Rocha2012] (secondary; paper not read). The octopus does not mimic the jawfish.

## 3. The overall scientific debate

### 3.1 What the founding paper claims, and how strongly

[Norman2001] is a four-page observational report: nine adults, 16 days of diving, about 6 hours of video. Its claims, in the authors' words:

- Several postures "are clearly impersonations of venomous animals co-occurring in this habitat" (abstract).
- But in the Results: "Human interpretations of potential mimicries are subjective, and at this stage we are only confident in our interpretation of the mimicry of soles, lion-fishes and banded sea-snakes."
- Facultative use: "our observations suggest that the octopus makes decisions about the most appropriate form of mimicry to use" — supported by one line of evidence, the sea-snake display occurring only after damselfish attacks (four events).
- They state plainly that it "is not known whether the mimic octopus is a poisonous (Müllerian) mimic or a non-toxic (Batesian) mimic".
- They consider and set aside two alternatives: convergent evolution of banding, and courtship display misread as mimicry (no conspecifics were present; both sexes do it).
- They note that still images "cannot convey the movements that are the most convincing aspect of these displays".

So the original paper itself supports three targets with confidence and two (anemone, jellyfish) only as possibilities. Everything beyond that entered through media coverage.

### 3.2 Hanlon, Conroy & Forsythe 2008 (abstract only, plus secondary summaries)

What the abstract establishes (close paraphrase):

- Starting point: the Indonesian "mimic octopuses" are "reputed" to mimic up to 13 species. The study set out to observe what the animals actually do, filming two habituated species continuously during natural foraging.
- Strong quantitative support for one behaviour: flounder mimicry, nearly 500 episodes in five days, by both species.
- Flounder mimicry is tied to movement across open sand, and the authors treat mimicry together with camouflage as the *primary* defence.
- When motionless: resemblance to small sponges, tube-worm tubes or colonial tunicates.
- In interactions with fishes and stomatopods: ordinary secondary defences, not mimicry.

What secondary sources say about the body of the paper (not verified by me):

- The authors agree that the octopuses mimic flounders, but argue the broader case for mimicry has been "overrated" [Faulkes2009] (blog; the word is quoted there from the paper).
- No predation event on an octopus was recorded in 189 hours of video; the study was observational, with no experiments and no collection permitted; the local flounders are poorly known, so the benefit of resembling them is unclear [Faulkes2009].
- Hanlon's earlier position in the press: only flatfish mimicry proved conclusively [CSM2002].
- [Huffard2010] cite Hanlon et al. for the point that the lack of a conclusive flatfish model is a recognised weakness, and that the species "frequently challenges the distinction between mimicry and crypsis".

**Limitation to pass on**: I cannot state from first-hand reading whether the 2008 study observed, failed to observe, or explicitly disputed the sea-snake and lionfish displays. The abstract is silent on both. Anyone needing that point must read the paper.

### 3.3 Huffard et al. 2010 (full text read)

- Language throughout is hedged: "may involve facultative imperfect mimicry"; "believed to represent"; "possible mimicry"; "possibly mimicry".
- Flatfish swimming is called "the most widely publicized though insufficiently analysed example of conspicuous defence in octopuses".
- Phylogenetic result: flatfish swimming is shared by a clade of long-armed sand octopuses and appears to have evolved together with very long arms, not as a later re-use of an older swimming style. What is special to *T. mimicus* is the *conspicuous* high-contrast colour worn while doing it.
- Three competing explanations for that conspicuous colour are left open: mimicry of a toxic sole; disruptive coloration against black sand with white shell fragments; honest warning (aposematic) coloration if the octopus is itself unpalatable.
- Inheritance: a laboratory-reared *Macrotritopus defilippi* that had never seen a flatfish performed flatfish swimming, which suggests the behaviour is innate rather than copied from observation [Huffard2010, citing Hanlon2010].
- They argue imperfect resemblance to several models can still work if it makes a predator hesitate.

### 3.4 Are the models toxic, and do they occur with the octopus?

| Model | Dangerous? | Sympatric? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Banded sea krait, *Laticauda* | Yes — venom injected through fangs [Norman2001]; "venomous" [Huffard2010] | Stated as co-occurring [Norman2001] | Species not identified |
| Lionfish, *Pterois* | Yes — venomous spines [Norman2001] | Stated as co-occurring [Norman2001] | Genus level only |
| Banded sole, *Zebrias* | **Uncertain.** [Norman2001] state that soles "including Zebrias species" have poison glands at the fin bases. [Huffard2010] list *Pardachirus pavoninus* as toxic but *Zebrias* only as "potentially" toxic | "abundant sole found in this habitat" [Norman2001] | The best-documented toxic sole (*Pardachirus*) is not the one named in 2001 |
| Flounders, *Bothus* spp. | No — "non-toxic flounders" [Huffard2010]. The Atlantic model *Bothus lunatus* was reported non-toxic [Hanlon2010] (secondary: via an annotated bibliography; abstract does not state it) | Common and overlapping in range [Huffard2010] | If flounders are the model, the mimicry is not Batesian in the classic sense |
| Sand anemone, *Megalactis* | Stinging cells [Norman2001] | Implied | Offered only "such as" |
| Jellyfish | Not specified | "found in the region" [Norman2001] | No species |
| Sponges, tunicates, worm tubes | Not claimed to be dangerous | Yes — among the few objects on the sand [Hanlon2008] (abstract) | Background objects, not deterrent models |

Why a non-toxic flatfish might still be worth resembling: [Huffard2010] suggest some gape-limited predators cannot quickly swallow an adult flatfish, so looking like one may help even without a toxic model.

### 3.5 What has never been shown

- No experiment has tested whether any predator is deceived by any of the displays. [Norman2001] and [Hanlon2008] are observational.
- No measurement of resemblance (shape or colour statistics) exists [Huffard2010].
- The octopus's own toxicity or palatability "remains to be tested" [Huffard2010]. One anecdote of a flounder taking and rejecting a mimic octopus hints at unpalatability [Huffard2010, citing NormanHochberg2005]. If true, the banded pattern could be an honest warning signal rather than mimicry.
- Whether individuals learn the displays or are born with them is unresolved for *T. mimicus*; the innate evidence comes from a different species.
- The idea that the octopus "chooses" a mimic to suit the predator rests on four sea-snake events.

### 3.6 Fair one-paragraph summary for the site

The mimic octopus certainly changes its shape, colour and movement in striking ways, and scientists agree that its flat, undulating swimming closely resembles a flatfish. Resemblance to a banded sea snake has been filmed and described, and resemblance to a lionfish has been reported by the discoverers but is less well illustrated. Whether these displays actually fool predators, and whether the octopus is copying dangerous animals in particular, has not been tested experimentally. Other items often listed — anemone, jellyfish, sponge, tunicate, worm tube — are either tentative suggestions or stationary camouflage, and stingray and snake eel do not appear in the scientific literature that could be found.

## 5. Caveats the website should display

1. Only three impersonations (flatfish, lionfish, sea snake) were claimed with confidence by the discoverers; only the flatfish has been confirmed and quantified by an independent team.
2. No experiment has shown that predators are fooled. "Mimicry" here means documented resemblance plus a plausible, untested function.
3. Anemone and jellyfish were offered as possibilities from few observations; sponge, tunicate and worm-tube resemblances are stationary camouflage; stingray and snake eel have no scientific source that could be found.
4. The flatfish model is not settled (banded sole vs. various flounders), and not all proposed models are toxic.
5. Whether the octopus itself is toxic or unpalatable is unknown, so "harmless animal copying dangerous ones" is a hypothesis, not a finding.
6. Popular counts of 13–18 imitated species are not supported by the scientific papers.
7. Several animations are necessarily reconstructions and should be labelled as such (see section 4).
