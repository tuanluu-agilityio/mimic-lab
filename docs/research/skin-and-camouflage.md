## 1. Chromatophore organs [General]

### 1.1 Structure

From Messenger (2001), abstract read:

- Cephalopod chromatophores are **organs, not single cells**. They are neuromuscular and are **not controlled by hormones**, unlike the chromatophores of fish, amphibians or crustaceans.
- Each organ is an **elastic sacculus containing pigment**, with a set of **radial muscles** attached around its edge, each muscle with its own nerves and glia.
- When the radial muscles contract they pull the sacculus out into a flat disc and the spot of colour **expands**. When they relax, elastic energy stored in the sacculus pulls it back to a small dot.

### 1.2 Neural control

- The radial muscles are driven by motoneurons whose cell bodies are in the **chromatophore lobes** of the brain (in the suboesophageal mass). Control is hierarchical: the optic lobes, acting mainly on what the animal sees, select a body pattern; the chromatophore lobes execute it. (Messenger 2001, abstract read.)
- The axons run from the brain to the skin without a synapse on the way, which is why change is so fast. [Background; Messenger 2001]
- Nerve fibres innervate groups of chromatophores, so the skin is organised into small patches that change together. (Messenger 2001, abstract read.)

### 1.3 Speed

- Change takes a fraction of a second. Reviews commonly describe expansion in hundreds of milliseconds. [Background] I did not verify an exact figure in a source this session, so the site should say "in a fraction of a second" and not quote a number.
- Octopuses can match background "almost instantly" (Huffard et al. 2010, citing Packard 1972; full text read).

### 1.4 Pigment colours in octopus skin

- In *Octopus vulgaris* the chromatophores "may be yellow, orange, red or black", with the colour classes interspersed in a regular arrangement. (Froesch & Messenger 1978, abstract read.)
- Cephalopod chromatophore pigments are ommochromes; black/brown, red, orange and yellow are the available classes. There are no blue or green pigments. Blues, greens and white come from the structural layers below. [Background; Messenger 2001; Mäthger et al. 2009]
- Pigment is held in nanostructured granules inside the pigment cell; in squid, reflectin protein in the sheath cells around the pigment cell adds some structural colour to the expanded chromatophore itself. (Williams et al. 2019, abstract read; squid, not octopus.)
- Which pigment classes *T. mimicus* has is not published. [Unverified] Its visible palette (dark brown, pale cream/white) is consistent with dark chromatophores over a white reflecting layer.

## 2. Reflecting cells [General]

From Mäthger, Denton, Marshall & Hanlon (2009), abstract read, plus Froesch & Messenger (1978), abstract read.

### 2.1 Iridophores

- Cells containing stacks of thin platelets. Colour is **structural**: light reflecting from the regularly spaced platelets interferes, giving blues, greens, pinks and silvery or metallic effects that shift with viewing angle. The platelets are made of protein (reflectins). [General]
- **Controllability.** "Most iridescence and white scattering is produced by passive reflectance." Active change is documented for **some iridophores in squid**, through a cholinergic, non-synaptic system, and it is slow (seconds to minutes) compared with chromatophores. (Mäthger et al. 2009; Wardill et al. 2012 and Izumi et al. 2010 for the squid mechanism, titles verified only.)
- **In octopuses** iridophores are regarded as passive: no physiological control has been shown. Their appearance changes only because the chromatophores above them cover or uncover them. [Background; consistent with Mäthger et al. 2009 restricting active control to squid] The blue rings of *Hapalochlaena* are the well-known octopus case of iridophore colour being displayed, and that display is produced by muscles and chromatophores around the iridophores, not by tuning the iridophores themselves [Background, not verified this session].

### 2.2 Leucophores

- Cells that **scatter all visible wavelengths**, so they look white in white light and take on the colour of whatever light falls on them. [General]
- In *Octopus vulgaris* they are much-branched cells covered in small club-shaped bodies; some tens of them form a "sponge" in the centre of each skin unit, which "reflects light over a broad band and may be important in camouflage". (Froesch & Messenger 1978, abstract read.)
- They are passive. No physiological control is known in octopuses. [Background]
- Because they reflect ambient light, leucophores (and to a degree iridophores) give the skin a baseline that follows the colour of the surroundings without the animal needing to sense colour. [Hypothesis; see section 5]

## 3. Layering order in the dermis [General]

From surface inward:

1. **Epidermis** (thin, transparent).
2. **Chromatophore organs**: the most superficial colour layer. Within it, in several species, the darker chromatophores lie deeper than the yellow ones [Background, not verified this session].
3. **Iridophores**.
4. **Leucophores**: the deepest reflecting layer, forming the white base.
5. Dermal muscle and connective tissue, then the body-wall muscle.

Support for this order: in *O. vulgaris* the "chromatic unit" contains chromatophores, iridophores and leucophores, with the chromatophores "disposed in a precise way with respect to the leucophores, the black ones lying above holes in the sponge" (Froesch & Messenger 1978, abstract read). The general ordering chromatophores above reflecting cells is the standard account (Messenger 2001 abstract: chromatophores are positioned precisely with respect to "reflecting structures beneath them").

How the layers combine: expanded dark chromatophores hide the reflecting layers and the skin looks dark; retracted chromatophores expose the leucophores and iridophores and the skin looks pale or iridescent. Intermediate states filter the reflected light.

**Model guidance.** Three stacked sheets is an honest schematic: pigment discs on top that grow and shrink, an iridescent platelet layer, and a white scattering layer at the bottom. Label it "based on *Octopus vulgaris* and other cephalopods".

## 4. Papillae and texture change [General]

- Octopuses and cuttlefish can raise the skin into bumps, ridges and spikes (papillae) and flatten it again to smooth, within about a second. The papillae are **muscular hydrostats in the dermis**: dermal erector muscles contract against the skin's own tissue with no rigid support. (Allen et al. 2014, *Journal of Morphology*, title verified; content from background knowledge, not re-read.)
- They are under direct neural control, like chromatophores. In cuttlefish the motoneurons run through the stellate ganglion and the papillae can be held raised for long periods. (Gonzalez-Bellido et al. 2018, title verified only.)
- In *T. mimicus*: the skin is described as relatively smooth, with raised papillae over the eyes and around the mantle margin. [Tm-secondary; fan-club page, unsourced] No primary description of its papillae was accessible. The white leucophore "sponge" at the centre of each *O. vulgaris* skin unit "can be elevated above the peripheral region" (Froesch & Messenger 1978), which is the general mechanism for raised white spots.

## 5. Apparent colour-blindness [General] and proposed explanations [Hypothesis]

### 5.1 The finding

- Behavioural tests found no colour discrimination in *Octopus* (Messenger, Wilson & Hedge 1973; Messenger 1977; titles verified only).
- Cuttlefish camouflage tests on coloured checkerboards gave the same answer: they respond to contrast, not hue (Marshall & Messenger 1996, "Colour-blind camouflage", title verified; Mäthger et al. 2006, title verified).
- Huffard et al. (2010, full text read) summarise the position as "octopuses are functionally colour-blind", citing those two papers.
- Most coleoid cephalopods have a single visual pigment in the retina [Background]. The firefly squid is the well-known exception [Background, not verified this session].
- Not tested in *T. mimicus*.

Wording for the site: "Octopuses tested so far appear to be colour-blind in the usual sense: they have one type of photoreceptor. How they still manage to match colours is not settled."

### 5.2 Proposed explanations (all hypotheses)

1. **The skin does the colour matching passively.** Leucophores and iridophores reflect whatever light is in the environment, so the pale parts of the pattern take on the ambient colour automatically, and the animal only needs to control brightness and pattern with its chromatophores. [Hypothesis; discussed in Mäthger et al. 2009 and related work]
2. **Chromatic aberration with odd-shaped pupils.** Stubbs & Stubbs (2016, abstract read) propose that the off-axis U- and W-shaped pupils let different wavelengths focus at different distances, so the animal could extract spectral information by focusing, "without requiring spectrally distinct photoreceptor classes". This is a physical-optics model, not a behavioural demonstration. It was criticised in a published reply the same year (Gagnon et al. 2016, *PNAS*; cited from memory, not verified this session).
3. **Light-sensitive skin.** Excised skin of *Octopus bimaculoides* expands its chromatophores in response to light, fastest in blue light (peak about 480 nm), and r-opsin is expressed in skin sensory neurons. (Ramirez & Oakley 2015, abstract read.) Phototransduction components are also found in cuttlefish and squid skin (Kingston et al. 2015, title verified only). The measured sensitivity closely matches the eye's opsin, so the skin appears to use the same single pigment; this shows the skin senses light, not that it distinguishes colours.
4. **Polarisation and contrast cues.** Cephalopods are sensitive to polarised light (Mäthger et al. 2009, abstract read), and good camouflage against predators' eyes may depend more on matching brightness, contrast and texture than hue. [Hypothesis]

Do not present any of these as "the answer".

## 6. What is documented for *T. mimicus* colouration

### 6.1 The pattern

- Base components recorded in the species description: a **white teardrop ring on the mid-dorsal mantle** and a **white "U" patch on the posterio-dorsal mantle**. [Tm-primary, abstract; Norman & Hochberg 2005]
- **High-contrast dark brown and light cream/white** body pattern, with **distinct arm bands**. [Tm-primary; Huffard et al. 2010, full text]
- When foraging by crawling, the animal is in "drab brown colours". In the sea-snake display two arms are "banded". The majority of individuals "also used general background camouflage patterns" in response to some passing predatory fishes. [Tm-primary; Norman et al. 2001, full text]

### 6.2 Permanent or changeable?

**Changeable.** The evidence:

- "These behaviours are neurally controlled rather than anatomically fixed. *Thaumoctopus mimicus* can regulate conspicuousness." (Huffard et al. 2010.)
- "Like its relatives, this octopus is capable of demonstrating excellent crypsis and polyphenism; however, *T. mimicus* frequently increases its conspicuousness through the intensification of a high-contrast body colour pattern." (Huffard et al. 2010.)
- During flounder mimicry octopuses were "actively moving and conspicuous, whereas immediately before and after flounder mimicry, they were camouflaged and motionless". When motionless they took on patterns and postures resembling small sponges, tube-worm tubes or colonial tunicates. (Hanlon et al. 2008, abstract read. This study covered two species together, *T. mimicus* and the undescribed "White V"/"blandopus" octopus.)
- Huffard et al. (2010) treat the high-contrast pattern as something that can be **visible at rest** in *T. mimicus* and *Wunderpus*, as well as intensified in displays.

So the correct description is: a characteristic brown-and-white banded pattern that the animal shows often and can intensify, fade or replace with camouflage. It is not a permanent paint job.

One primary-source disagreement to keep in mind: Huffard et al. (2010) say the other flatfish-swimming octopuses "remain camouflaged during flatfish swimming" while *T. mimicus* is consistently conspicuous; Hanlon et al. (2008) describe both of their species as conspicuous during flounder mimicry mainly because they are moving, with flounder colouration only "sometimes". The site can safely say that *T. mimicus* usually shows its high-contrast banding while swimming like a flatfish.

Interpretations of the banding, all [Hypothesis] (Huffard et al. 2010): mimicry of a toxic banded sole, disruptive colouration against black sand flecked with white shell fragments, or warning colouration. The toxicity of *T. mimicus* "remains to be tested".

### 6.3 Comparison with *Wunderpus photogenicus*

| | *Thaumoctopus mimicus* | *Wunderpus photogenicus* |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | Dark brown and cream/white; banded arms; white teardrop ring and white "U" on mantle | "A dramatic and fixed color pattern of white bars and spots over a brown-red background" |
| Fixed or changeable | Changeable; can be cryptic or conspicuous | Pattern layout described as **fixed**; most pronounced when the animal is disturbed or threatened |
| Eyes | Raised papilla over each eye [Tm-secondary] | "Small eyes on elongate stalks, a long, conical papilla over each eye" |
| Activity | Day-active | Emerges at dusk and dawn |
| Flatfish swimming | Yes | No. Swims dorsoventrally compressed but without imitating a flatfish |
| Individual identification | Not reported | Individually unique body patterns allow photo-identification (Huffard et al. 2008, title verified only) |

Sources: Norman & Hochberg 2005 (abstract); Hochberg, Norman & Finn 2006 (abstract); Huffard et al. 2010 (full text, including the statement that *W. photogenicus* uses dorsoventrally compressed swimming "but not flatfish swimming").

Relationship: in the mitochondrial phylogeny of Huffard et al. (2010), *T. mimicus* "appears most closely related to the crepuscular *W. photogenicus*".

Practical note: the two are often confused in photographs. The safest textual distinctions from the sources I read are the eye stalks and long conical eye papillae of *Wunderpus*, its red-brown background with fixed white bars and spots, and the white teardrop ring and "U" on the mantle of *T. mimicus*. Popular guides also say *Wunderpus* has sharply edged white markings while the mimic's are more diffuse; I did not find that in a primary source [Unverified].

## 8. Where the site must say "generalised from other octopus species"

- Chromatophore organ structure, control and speed (squid, cuttlefish, *Octopus vulgaris*).
- Pigment colour classes (*O. vulgaris*).
- Iridophores and leucophores, and the layering order (*O. vulgaris*, cuttlefish, squid).
- Papillae mechanism (other octopuses and cuttlefish).
- Colour-blindness and all proposed explanations (*Octopus vulgaris*, *O. bimaculoides*, cuttlefish).

Species-specific and safe to state for *T. mimicus*: the brown and white banded pattern, the white teardrop ring and "U" mantle marks, the fact that the pattern is neurally controlled and changeable, drab brown foraging colour, and the comparison with *Wunderpus*.
