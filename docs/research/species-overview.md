# Thaumoctopus mimicus — species overview

Research date: 2026-11-13. Citation keys refer to `scientific-references.md`.

**How to read the access tags.** Every statement carries a citation key. Where the source was not read in full, a tag follows the key:

- no tag = I read the full text of the source
- `(abstract only)` = I read only the abstract
- `(secondary)` = I did not open the source; the statement comes from another source that summarises it (named)
- `(database)` = taken from a database record, not a paper

Two primary papers were read in full: [Norman2001] and [Huffard2010]. The original species description [NormanHochberg2005] and the main field study [Hanlon2008] are paywalled; only their abstracts were read. Details that would normally come from the body of those two papers (type locality, holotype, measurements) are therefore marked as not verified.

Notes:

- Before the formal description, the animal was referred to simply as the "mimic octopus", "new to science", with the description "currently in process" [Norman2001].
- [Huffard2010] cites the description as "Norman & Hochberg, 2006" in its text and reference list, but gives the authority as "Norman & Hochberg, 2005" in its abstract. The publisher and Crossref both give 2005; use 2005.
- Diagnostic characters given in the abstract (close paraphrase): no calamus on the copulatory organ; no enlarged suckers in either sex; long narrow arms capable of arm autotomy at a set level near the base; base colour-pattern components including a white teardrop ring on the mid-dorsal mantle and a white "U" patch on the posterior dorsal mantle [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only).
- Closest relative in the only molecular phylogeny found: *Wunderpus photogenicus*. Together with the undescribed "White V" octopus and the "Hawaiian Long-Armed Sand Octopus" they form what the authors call the "Long-Armed Sand Octopus" (LASO) clade, sister to *Abdopus*. The authors suggest *Wunderpus* could be treated as a synonym of *Thaumoctopus*; this is a recommendation in that paper, not an adopted change [Huffard2010].

## 2. Type locality

**Not verified.** The type locality and holotype details are in the body of [NormanHochberg2005], which I could not open (paywalled; the Plazi/ZooBank treatment is a stub with no text). The abstract does not state them. Do not print a type locality on the site until someone reads the paper. What can be said safely:

- The species was first found off Sulawesi, Indonesia, in 1998 [Norman2001].
- The 2001 field observations were made off the northern coast of Sulawesi and at Gilimanuk, Bali [Norman2001].

## 3. Geographic distribution

- Original description: "primarily from the Indo-Malayan Archipelago from New Caledonia to Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines"; photographic records from the Red Sea extend the range to the western Indian Ocean (close paraphrase) [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only).
- Earlier statement, now superseded: "appears to be restricted to the Indo-Malayan archipelago" [Norman2001].
- Described as found "on sand plains throughout the Indo-West Pacific" [Huffard2010].
- Great Barrier Reef, Australia: a sighting off Lizard Island in 2012 was published as the first documented Australian record [Coker2013] (secondary: publisher abstract as returned by a web search; I could not open the page). Two different dates circulate (4 June 2012 in the abstract summary, 4 July 2012 in a Scientific American blog); not resolved.
- Gulf of Thailand: photographic/specimen record [Nabhitabhata2007] (secondary: citation taken from the reference list of other sources; paper not read). A Scientific American blog from 2014 describes a small female taken from soft bottom at about 65 ft in the upper Gulf of Thailand [SciAmThailand2014].
- Japan (Okinawa): GBIF indexes one preserved specimen, NSMT 76149, Oura, Nago, Okinawa, collected 2008-02-23 [GBIF] (database; identification not independently checked).
- Wikipedia additionally lists the Gulf of Oman; I found no primary source for that and it is not repeated here as fact [WikipediaMimic] (secondary, unverified).

## 4. Habitat

- Substrate: silt and sand "off river mouths"; soft sediment [Norman2001]; "soft sediment substrates" [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only); "open, featureless volcanic sand" at the North Sulawesi study site [Hanlon2008] (abstract only); black sand flecked with white shell fragments [Huffard2010].
- The habitat is rich in burrowing infauna (worms, echinoderms, crustaceans, fishes), with the sea floor riddled by burrows, tunnels and mounds [Norman2001].
- Few sessile objects are present; small sponges, tube-worm tubes and colonial tunicates are "among the few objects" in this habitat [Hanlon2008] (abstract only).
- Depth:
  - 2–12 m at the 2001 study sites [Norman2001].
  - 0.5–37 m according to the Crossref-deposited abstract of the species description [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only). **Discrepancy:** the older HTML abstract page on the publisher's site reads "0.5-7 m". The two versions of the same abstract disagree; the paper body was not available to settle it. 37 m is consistent with the roughly 20 m (65 ft) Gulf of Thailand record, 7 m is not.

## 5. Size

- Arm span up to 60 cm; one large female had a 60 cm arm span [Norman2001].
- "2-foot long" [AMNH2015] (museum outreach page; consistent with the 60 cm arm span).
- Arms are "very long" — more than 6.5 times mantle length is the threshold used for the long-armed category that includes this species [Huffard2010].
- Mantle length: a figure of about 58 mm appears in SeaLifeBase [SeaLifeBase] (secondary: seen only in a search-result snippet; page returned HTTP 403). Not verified against the description.

## 6. Diet and foraging

- Prey: small fish and crustaceans [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only); "mainly subterranean crustaceans and fishes" [Norman2001].
- Foraging mode: crawls along the bottom in drab brown colours, probing holes with the arm tips and using flared webs to trap fleeing prey ("speculative foraging") [Norman2001].
- Tunnel foraging: animals entered a tunnel completely and emerged from another hole up to 1 m away; the authors knew of no other octopus foraging through subterranean tunnels [Norman2001].
- Two tactile feeding tactics are reported from North Sulawesi [Hanlon2008] (abstract only; the tactics are not named in the abstract).

## 7. Activity pattern

- Day-active: emerges during daylight to forage on open sand "in full view of pelagic fish predators" [Norman2001]; "day-active" [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only).
- Foraging occurred twice daily at the North Sulawesi site [Hanlon2008] (abstract only).
- Its sister species *Wunderpus photogenicus* is described as crepuscular, in contrast [Huffard2010].

## 8. Burrow / den use

- Typically encountered sitting in the mouths of burrows on sand or silt mounds ("sentinel state", Fig. 1a) [Norman2001].
- Dens and food did not appear to be limiting at the North Sulawesi site, and *T. mimicus* shared habitat, dens, food and activity period with a second small long-armed octopus there [Hanlon2008] (abstract only).
- Individuals were well separated, 50–100 m apart [Norman2001].

## 9. Colour pattern and other biology relevant to the site

- Normal foraging colour: drab brown [Norman2001].
- Conspicuous pattern: high-contrast dark brown and light cream/white, with distinct arm bands; in this species (and *Wunderpus*) the high-contrast pattern is visible at rest, not only in the alarm display [Huffard2010].
- Can also be fully cryptic: "capable of demonstrating excellent crypsis" [Huffard2010]; most individuals used background camouflage in response to certain passing predatory fishes such as the trevally *Gnathanodon speciosus* (spelled "Gnathodon" in the paper) [Norman2001].
- Arm autotomy at a set level near the arm base [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only).
- Octopuses are functionally colour-blind; colour matching is achieved without colour vision [Huffard2010, citing Marshall & Messenger 1996 and Mäthger et al. 2006].
- Toxicity/palatability of *T. mimicus* itself: unknown and untested. A flounder is reported to have taken and then rejected one as prey, which suggests it may be unpalatable [Huffard2010, citing NormanHochberg2005].

## 10. Discovery history

- 1998: discovered off the coast of Sulawesi, Indonesia [Norman2001]; [AMNH2015].
- October 1998 – October 2000: nine adults observed over 16 days of daylight dives (northern Sulawesi; Gilimanuk, Bali); stills and more than 6 hours of video obtained [Norman2001].
- 2000: footage incorporated into a BBC/Discovery natural-history film [Norman2001].
- 2001: first scientific paper, introducing the term "dynamic mimicry" [Norman2001].
- 2005: formal description as a new genus and species [NormanHochberg2005] (abstract only).
- 2008: five-day continuous field study of foraging and mimicry off North Sulawesi [Hanlon2008] (abstract only).
- 2010: molecular phylogeny placing it with other long-armed sand octopuses [Huffard2010].
- 2011–2012: a jawfish (*Stalix* cf. *histrio*) filmed in Lembeh Strait swimming among a mimic octopus's arms, published as "opportunistic mimicry" by the fish [Rocha2012] (secondary: press coverage and AMNH; paper not read).

## 11. Conservation status

- IUCN Red List: **Least Concern**, assessor A.L. Allcock, assessed 13 July 2016, published in Red List version 2019-2 [IUCN2019] (secondary: the IUCN page returned HTTP 403. Assessor, assessment date and DOI are confirmed from the Crossref record of the assessment; the category "Least Concern" comes from Wikipedia's citation of the assessment and a SeaLifeBase search snippet). Check the IUCN page directly before publishing the category.
- No information on population trend or threats was verified. Claims on popular sites about aquarium-trade collection were not traced to a primary source and are not repeated here.
