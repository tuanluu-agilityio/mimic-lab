## 1. Locomotion modes in octopuses

### 1.1 Crawling [General, *Octopus vulgaris*]

From Levy & Hochner (2017, open-access full text read via summary), which reviews Levy, Flash & Hochner (2015):

- Suckers on an arm anchor to the bottom and a section of the arm near the body **elongates, pushing** the animal away. Crawling is done by pushing, not pulling.
- Each arm pushes in a fixed direction set by where it attaches around the body. The direction of travel is the sum of the pushes from whichever arms are active.
- The body can face one way while travelling another: the octopus "can locomote in any direction relative to its facing direction".
- There is **no regular stepping rhythm**. Arm use shows "no apparent order", and the authors conclude there is no central pattern generator for crawling. Arms are chosen moment to moment.
- The head and the line between the eyes stay close to horizontal during crawling.

In *T. mimicus*: "Foraging individuals typically crawled along the substrate in drab brown colours." [Tm-primary; Norman et al. 2001] The mechanics have not been analysed in this species.

### 1.2 Bipedal walking [General; not documented in *T. mimicus*]

- Two species "walk on two alternating arms using a rolling gait and appear to use the remaining six arms for camouflage": *Octopus marginatus* (now *Amphioctopus marginatus*), which then resembles a coconut, and *Abdopus aculeatus*, which resembles a clump of floating algae. Each walking arm was on the sand for more than half the stride, which qualifies it as walking. (Huffard, Boneka & Full 2005, abstract read.)
- In *A. aculeatus*, bipedal locomotion "proceeded as fast as swimming" and counts as a fast escape that keeps elements of disguise. (Huffard 2006, abstract read.)
- **I found no report of bipedal walking in *T. mimicus*.** Do not animate it for this species, or label it clearly as another species' behaviour.

### 1.3 Jet propulsion through the funnel

Mechanics [Background; Wells 1990; Trueman & Packard 1968; neither re-read this session]:

1. The mantle expands and water enters the mantle cavity around the mantle edge.
2. The mantle edge seals against the head and funnel.
3. Circular muscles of the mantle contract, driven through the stellate ganglia, and water is forced out through the narrow funnel.
4. The animal moves in the direction opposite to the jet.

Direction control [Background]: the funnel is a flexible muscular tube and can be aimed. Pointed forward (toward the arms) it drives the animal **backward, mantle first with arms trailing**, which is the usual fast escape. Bent back or to the side it gives forward or sideways movement.

Cost: jetting is physiologically expensive for octopuses. Huffard et al. (2010, full text read) refer to "the known physiological inefficiencies of jet-propelled locomotion in octopuses", citing Wells et al. (1987) and Wells (1990), and argue that long-armed species with a small mantle "are likely to exhibit disproportionally weaker jet-propulsive abilities".

In *T. mimicus*: "When moving faster (using jet propulsion), the octopus drew all its arms into a leaf-shaped wedge" [Tm-primary; Norman et al. 2001]. So the flatfish swim is jet-powered.

### 1.4 Swimming postures [General, *Abdopus aculeatus*]

- Huffard (2006, abstract read) compared crawling, bipedal walking, swimming and jetting in *A. aculeatus*. Body postures and patterns were more varied in slow crawling than in fast escapes. Octopuses "swam faster when dorsoventrally compressed, a form that may generate lift, than when swimming in the head-raised posture". Absolute speed was not correlated with body mass in any mode.
- **Dorsoventrally compressed (DVC) swimming** means swimming forward, eyes or arms first, with the head, mantle and arms flattened into one plane. It "may also be an efficient way for long-armed octopuses to swim" and "may incorporate lift". [Hypothesis; Huffard et al. 2010]

### 1.5 Medusoid swimming

- "Medusoid" swimming means opening the arms and web like a bell and closing them to push water, in the manner of a jellyfish. It is best documented in the finned deep-sea (cirrate) octopods and some pelagic octopods. [Background; not verified against a source this session]
- In *T. mimicus* there is one relevant observation: "a large female (arm span, 60 cm) swam to the sea surface from 4 m deep, then slowly sank with undulating arms spread evenly around the animal. This behaviour may impersonate large jellyfishes found in the region." [Tm-primary; Norman et al. 2001] Note what this says: a swim upward, then **passive sinking** with arms spread and undulating. It does not describe bell-like pulsing as propulsion, and the authors say they were not confident in interpreting it as mimicry.
- A secondary web summary attributes a slow arm-expanding "jellyfish" movement to Hanlon et al. (2008). [Tm-secondary; unchecked]
- Recommendation: do not list medusoid swimming as an established locomotion mode of *T. mimicus*. If shown, describe it as "sinking with arms spread, seen once and possibly resembling a jellyfish".

## 2. "Flatfish swimming" in *T. mimicus* and relatives

### 2.1 The original description [Tm-primary; Norman et al. 2001, full text]

> "When moving faster (using jet propulsion), the octopus drew all its arms into a leaf-shaped wedge with a central mantle trailing the head. In this form it swam between worm mounds, undulating the body in the fashion of a swimming flatfish."

The authors interpret this as mimicry of a banded sole (*Zebrias* spp.) that is abundant in the habitat. They state that they are confident only in the sole, lionfish and sea-snake interpretations.

### 2.2 The field study [Tm-primary; Hanlon et al. 2008, abstract only]

- Two species were followed and filmed while foraging on open volcanic sand off North Sulawesi: *T. mimicus* and an undescribed similar species ("White V" / "blandopus").
- "Mimicry of a local, abundant flounder occurred commonly during 5 days of natural foraging: nearly 500 episodes were analysed."
- "Both octopus species mimicked the shape, swimming actions, speed, duration, and sometimes the coloration of swimming flounders."
- "The key finding was that octopuses used flounder mimicry only when their movement would give away camouflage in this open habitat." Before and after, they were camouflaged and motionless (sitting or crawling very slowly).
- Mimicry was used as a primary defence. In filmed interactions with fishes and stomatopods, the octopuses used ordinary secondary defences, not mimicry.

Caution on a widely repeated figure: Wikipedia says nearly 500 instances were "seen by a single individual". The abstract says nearly 500 episodes were analysed over 5 days across two species. Use the abstract's wording.

### 2.3 The definition used in later work [Tm-primary; Huffard et al. 2010, full text]

> "'Flatfish swimming' comprises the following behavioural elements: similar swimming durations and speeds to that of flatfish, arms positioned to attain flatfish shape, both eyes positioned prominently as flatfish eyes, and undulating movements of arms during swimming that resemble fin movements of flatfish (Hanlon et al., 2008)."

Further points from the same paper:

- Reported so far for *T. mimicus*, *Macrotritopus defilippi* (Atlantic), the "White V" octopus and the "Hawaiian Long-Armed Sand Octopus".
- "Unlike the latter three octopuses, which remain camouflaged during flatfish swimming, *T. mimicus* consistently incorporates conspicuousness, with a high-contrast dark brown and light cream body-colour pattern."
- DVC swimming "is an essential aspect of flatfish swimming", but DVC alone is not flatfish swimming: in plain DVC "the entire head and mantle may be raised rather than just the eyes, and the arms need not undulate like flatfish fins". *Abdopus aculeatus* and *Wunderpus photogenicus* use DVC "but not flatfish swimming".
- Very long arms (defined as 6.5 times mantle length or more) "may allow the better imitation of undulating flatfish fins".
- Evolutionary conclusion: flatfish swimming "appears to have evolved concurrently with extremely long arms, in a clade of sand-dwelling species", contrary to the earlier prediction (Huffard 2006) that it reused an existing swimming style. The conspicuous colour pattern of *T. mimicus* "may represent a secondary adaptation". [Hypothesis, based on a mitochondrial-gene phylogeny]
- The models are uncertain: possibly a suite of flatfishes (Soleidae and Bothidae), toxic and non-toxic, i.e. "imperfect mimicry of multiple models". [Hypothesis]
- Flatfish swimming appeared in a laboratory-reared *M. defilippi* "that had never seen a flatfish", which suggests the behaviour is inherited (citing Hanlon, Watson & Barbosa 2010).

### 2.4 What the sources agree on, for animation

The direction of travel relative to the body is not stated in the passages I could read. Huffard et al. (2010) describe DVC as forward, "eyes or arms-first", swimming. Norman et al. (2001) say the mantle trails the head. Taken together the animal travels head-first with the mantle behind and the arms folded back along the sides, but confirm against the video in Norman et al.'s electronic appendix or Hanlon et al. (2008) before animating direction.

### 2.5 The Atlantic counterpart [General, *Macrotritopus defilippi*]

Hanlon, Watson & Barbosa (2010, abstract read): *M. defilippi* was filmed in five Caribbean locations "mimicking the swimming behavior (posture, style, speed, duration) and coloration" of the flounder *Bothus lunatus*. It used flounder mimicry "only during swimming, when their movement would give away camouflage". The full text, which I could not open, is the most likely place to find measured swimming speeds for a flatfish-swimming octopus.

## 3. Burrow use and sand-flat foraging in *T. mimicus*

### 3.1 Habitat [Tm-primary]

- Silt and sand off river mouths, 2–12 m deep in the 2001 study; a habitat "riddling the sea-floor with burrows, tunnels and mounds" made by worms, echinoderms, crustaceans and fishes. (Norman et al. 2001.)
- Species depth range 0.5–37 m on soft sediment. (Norman & Hochberg 2005, abstract.)
- Later records in very shallow water: under 30 cm deep on a tidal sand flat in Mozambique. (Marshall, Conradie & Gilroy 2024, abstract read.)
- Individuals in the 2001 study were "well separated (50–100 m apart)".

### 3.2 Burrows and dens [Tm-primary]

- "Mimic octopuses were typically encountered sitting in the mouths of burrows on sand or silt mounds." The figure caption calls this the "sentinel state in mouth of burrow". (Norman et al. 2001.)
- In the sea-snake display, "six arms were threaded down a hole and two were raised in opposite directions, banded, curled and undulated". Seen four times, each time in response to attacks by territorial damselfishes. (Norman et al. 2001.)
- "Dens and food were not limiting"; several small long-armed octopus species shared the same habitat, den sources, food and activity period. (Hanlon et al. 2008, abstract.)
- Whether *T. mimicus* digs its own burrow or uses holes made by other animals is not stated in what I read. [Unverified] An aquarist account says captive animals need a deep sand bed and can stay buried for days (Caldwell & Shaw, The Cephalopod Page; not peer reviewed).

### 3.3 Activity pattern [Tm-primary]

- Day-active: it "emerges during daylight hours to forage on sand substrates in full view of pelagic fish predators". (Norman et al. 2001; Norman & Hochberg 2005, abstract.)
- "Foraging occurred twice per day." (Hanlon et al. 2008, abstract.)

### 3.4 Foraging tactics [Tm-primary]

From Norman et al. (2001), full text:

1. **Arm probing.** Foraging animals crawl along the bottom "using their arm tips to probe down holes". The authors call this "typical speculative foraging (threading long arms down burrows and holes to seize fishes and crustacean prey)".
2. **Web trapping.** They use "the flared webs to trap fleeing prey".
3. **Tunnel foraging.** "Animals were observed to enter a tunnel completely and to emerge from another hole up to 1 m from the entrance point. We are unaware of any other octopuses that forage through subterranean tunnels."

From Hanlon et al. (2008), abstract: "two tactile feeding tactics were used". The abstract does not name them; they are presumably the probing and web-over tactics, but I could not confirm that. [Unverified]

Prey: small fishes and crustaceans (Norman & Hochberg 2005, abstract); "mainly subterranean crustaceans and fishes" (Norman et al. 2001).

### 3.5 About the term "speculative pounce"

- In the general octopus literature, a "speculative pounce" or "web-over" is when the octopus jumps onto a rock, clump or patch of sediment, spreads its web over it like a tent, and feels underneath with its arm tips for anything trapped. [Background; standard usage in Hanlon & Messenger 2018, not re-read]
- Norman et al. (2001) use "speculative foraging" for *T. mimicus* to mean threading arms down burrows, and separately mention flared webs trapping fleeing prey. They do not use the phrase "speculative pounce".
- The clearest published description of the web-over tactic in this group is for the close relative *Wunderpus photogenicus*: it catches prey "by flaring the arms and webs over patches of sand or coral rubble to trap enclosed (and typically buried) prey" and "also extends its arms into holes to probe". (Hochberg, Norman & Finn 2006, abstract read.)
- Recommended site wording for *T. mimicus*: "It hunts by touch: it pushes its long arm tips down holes and burrows, and spreads its web to trap animals that try to flee. It has even been seen entering a tunnel and coming out of another hole up to a metre away." This stays within what Norman et al. (2001) wrote.

## 4. Where the site must say "generalised from other octopus species"

- Crawling mechanics (*Octopus vulgaris*).
- Bipedal walking (*Amphioctopus marginatus*, *Abdopus aculeatus*); not reported in *T. mimicus*.
- Jet propulsion mechanics and funnel steering (octopuses and cephalopods generally).
- Lift and efficiency of flattened swimming (*Abdopus aculeatus*; hypothesis for long-armed species).
- Medusoid swimming (mainly deep-sea and pelagic octopods).
- The "speculative pounce" as a named behaviour (general octopus literature; described for *Wunderpus*).

Species-specific and safe to state for *T. mimicus*: crawling in drab brown while foraging; jet-powered flatfish swimming with arms in a leaf-shaped wedge and an undulating body; the high-contrast pattern during that swim; sitting in burrow mouths; arm-tip probing of holes; flared-web trapping; tunnel foraging up to 1 m; day activity; the single observation of sinking with arms spread.
